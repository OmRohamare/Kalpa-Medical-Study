## Individual write-up: PRITAM CHAVHAN, problem framing, business analysis and presentation

**My role.** I led how the project was framed, how the business analysis was structured, and how it was turned into the final presentation. The team’s job was to answer one hard question for management: why did booked tests grow about 5% against an 18% plan? My contribution was deciding what question to ask, how to cut the gap so it made business sense, which explanations deserved testing, and how to tell the story in six slides that a COO, finance controller and city heads could act on.

**Framing the problem.** Management planned about 18% growth in booked tests for January to June 2026 and delivered about 5%: 458,002 tests against a plan of 515,740, a gap of 57,738 and 88.8% attainment. Every one of the 36 city-months finished below plan. I framed the work around three questions: where did the shortfall come from, what drove the growth we did get, and what should management do. I also set one rule that shaped the whole deck: keep two numbers apart, the +21,700 year-on-year increase and the 57,738 gap to plan. Mixing them is the easiest way to mislead a board, so every slide states which one it means.

**Business analysis.** The key move was asking what a healthy business had actually delivered. The four cities with no system change (Ahmedabad, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune) grew +8.4% together and still missed a plan built for +18.2%. Against that benchmark, 42,798 tests, 74% of the gap, is plan ambition above what healthy cities achieved. The other 14,940 tests, 26%, is Chennai and Jaipur growing less than even that benchmark. Reading how the plan was built showed that each city’s target is last year’s month times one flat factor between 16% and 20%, with no driver build. The plan needed +25% over the late-2025 run-rate, and the business delivered about +11%. This reframed the headline from “the business underperformed” to “the plan asked for more than a healthy business delivered, and one narrow problem sits on top.”

I then explained the growth we did get. Active patients added 16,418 tests, tests per booking added 9,697, and booking frequency removed 4,415. I described these as an allocation of observed change, not causes. The revenue view added the business meaning: invoiced revenue grew 12.0% on 5.0% volume, because four package products supplied 87% of the increase while standard tests grew only 0.8%. I put an indicative value on the gap, about Rs 3.4 crore, labelled as not profit and not a forecast.

**Choosing which explanations to test.** I drove the list of twelve candidate explanations and sorted each as supported, ruled out or untestable. The decisive finding was timing. New patients in Chennai and Jaipur fell 27% and 34% year on year, while the peer cities rose 15% to 19%. The new booking system went live on 1 April, but the break started in January, and weekly tests showed no dip in the go-live week (4,595 before, 4,579 during). That ruled out the cutover, the explanation most people reach for first, and kept management from fixing the wrong thing. Retention (about 33% everywhere), clinic capacity, no-shows and delays also do not separate the two cities from the rest.

On the free home-collection campaign, I framed what the evidence can and cannot show. Home’s share of tests rose from 34% to 49%, then fell to 40% when the fee returned, while clinic tests fell 18.1% in Q1. The upper bound on extra tests is about 4,600. With no control group, I described it as volume moving between channels, not proven new demand.

**Fact, interpretation, hypothesis.** I set this discipline for the team. The size and location of the gap are facts. “The plan was top-down and ambitious” is an interpretation. What caused the January break, and what launched on 1 January, are hypotheses the data cannot test. The deck says plainly that we have not established why acquisition broke, and that price, competitor, marketing-spend and staffing effects are outside what the data contains.

**The presentation.** I owned the six-slide deck’s storyline and design. It runs as one argument: the business question, the gap split and driver tree, the headline claim, the evidence, the caveats and the decisions. I rewrote every slide title to state a conclusion, for example “The 57,738-test gap is mostly a planning question; the +21,700 of growth came from patients and packages.” I chose the visuals that carry the argument, namely the gap split, the driver tree, the waterfall and the new-patient index chart, and I wrote the speaker notes with evidence references. I also prepared 14 board questions with evidence-based answers, and a 37-claim evidence ledger so any number can be defended under challenge.

**Recommendations.** I turned the findings into three actions. First, diagnose the January break in Chennai and Jaipur within 30 days by rebuilding the acquisition funnel and asking local teams what changed. Second, hold acquisition spend until a cause is named, then pilot for 8 to 12 weeks with held-out control clinics and scale only if the pilot beats control. Third, re-base the plan bottom-up within 45 days by city, channel and driver. Owners and thresholds are proposals for management to confirm, and I made no promise of recovered tests or financial return.

**What I would do differently.** I would ask the Chennai and Jaipur city heads what changed in December and January before finalising, since that is the one question the data cannot answer. I would also request the plan documentation from finance, to turn the 74% finding from inference into fact.

**What I learned.** A strong recommendation can be to diagnose before spending, and saying clearly what we do not know made the argument more credible. I also learned that framing is analysis: the choice of benchmark, the split of the gap and the order of the slides decided what management would conclude.

**How it connects.** The data foundation and metrics gave every number a reliable base. My part was the framing, the business interpretation and the deck that turned them into a decision.
